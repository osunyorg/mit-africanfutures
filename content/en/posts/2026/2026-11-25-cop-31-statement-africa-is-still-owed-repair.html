A statement from the African Climate Reparations Collective on the outcome of COP31 

body: >-


COP31 closed on 22 November 2026 after some fifty hours of overtime, without the overarching political decision it was meant to deliver. The talks broke down as countries failed to agree on a proposed political decision that did not name fossil fuels, opened the door to carbon removal and capture technologies that could prolong their use, left adaptation finance without a baseline or a delivery plan , and failed to refer to the International Court of Justice's 2025 advisory opinion on States' climate obligations which has been welcomed by States through UN Resolution 80/263 . We stand with the African ministers, and the African Group of Negotiators, who strongly criticized and pushed back on the text.


Climate reparations were not on the agenda in Antalya, and for the communities we work with, this has been the problem all along. Six weeks ago in Mombasa, over 110 community members, frontline defenders, lawyers, economists and researchers met at the African Symposium on Climate Reparations. The discussions in Mombasa focused on what climate reparations should mean in practice, who bears responsibility for climate harm, and what affected communities need to secure justice and repair.  Fossil fuels have built wealth in the Global North and left the cost with the Global Majority. For more than a century, industrialised countries have accumulated wealth through fossil fuels  and the extraction of Africa's land, labour and resources. African communities have borne the costs of dispossession and environmental degradation, and now face disproportionate impacts from climate change. Conventional approaches often frame the problem as a financing gap: Africa lacks resources, so the task is to mobilise more money. But that leaves unanswered questions: why resources are lacking in the first place, how wealth continues to leave the continent, and who dictates the terms of financing. Africa contributes less than 4% of global emissions, yet it loses 2-5% of its GDP to climate extremes every year .Oil spills, gas flaring and mining have poisoned rivers and farmland, severed communities off from their forests and sacred places, and destroyed the ecosystems that people depend on for water, food and medicine. This is what a sacrifice zone looks like: a continent treated as a source of extraction for the benefit of others, then left to carry the consequences. Fossil fuel subsidies reached US$921 billion in 2024 , and the IMF estimates their hidden costs at up to US$6.7 trillion a year. Meanwhile the talks were co-presided by two fossil fuel producers. A transition away from fossil fuels is therefore a matter of justice as well as emissions. It must be fast and fair, and it must be paid for by those who benefited from the old system. The communities and ecosystems harmed by the same system and its legacies are owed more than just a lukewarm promise that the harm will eventually stop - they are owed repair. Loss and damage is where that debt becomes visible. The Fund for Responding to Loss and Damage has received 176 requests totalling US$2.8 billion , in its first funding call, many times what it can pay out. Its first planned emergency payout, US$21.6 million for Nepal, covers only about 0.5% of the recovery bill, demonstrating just how far the system falls short. Voluntary pledges alone cannot discharge States' legal obligations . Communities cannot wait for another extraordinary board meeting each time disaster strikes. Repair must be predictable, proportionate to the harm, and shaped by those who live with it. Repair must also account for what cannot be priced: ancestral land, language, sacred places, and ecosystems themselves, which the Collective recognises as rights-bearers. Finance that arrives as debt is not justice and deepens dependency. Adaptation finance has again been weakened at the moment it should have grown. Public climate finance is an obligation under Article 9.1 of the Paris Agreement . Yet wealthy countries pushed to widen the discussion to private finance, and new pledges to the Adaptation Fund totaled US$50.2 million , down from US$135 million at COP30 (a drop of 63%), and well below the fund's annual target of at least US$300 million, and the fund's broader goal of mobilizing US$1.2 billion from 2026 to 2029.  Africa is being asked to borrow in order to survive a crisis it did not cause. In nearly 80% of African countries, interest payments exceed public spending on health and education. Vulnerable countries are judged riskier, pay more to borrow, and sink deeper into the debt that leaves them less able to prepare for the next flood or drought. Reparative finance must break that cycle. It means grants without conditions, debt cancellation and suspension in times of crisis, African control over tax rules, an end to illicit financial flows, and reform of the institutions and credit ratings that decide who can afford to repair what has been broken. It also means finance that reaches communities directly, without intermediaries, on terms they can meet.  Forests cannot be saved by removing the people who protect them. We note the renewed backing for the Tropical Forest Forever Facility, which is welcome. However, a fund that pays per hectare protects nothing unless the money reaches those who steward the land. Many of Africa's forests, from the Congo Basin to the Mau, are held in trust by communities who have stewarded them for generations, often without recognition or compensation. Support must flow to them, secure their land rights, respect their right to say no, and not turn their territories into assets for credits and offsets. Conservation that displaces people, or green projects imposed without consent, repeat the extraction that created the crisis.  

Our call.

The Paris Agreement and earlier commitments remain in force, and so do the legal obligations behind them. The International Court of Justice has confirmed that breaches of States' climate obligations carry consequences, including cessation, non-repetition and full reparation. The task now is to turn these obligations into accountability and effective remedies. Ahead of the July 2027 conference on transitioning away from fossil fuels and COP32 in Addis Ababa, African governments must act together to advance a common position on climate reparations, grounded in the demands of affected communities. The African Call for Climate Reparations, which emerged from the Mombasa Symposium and has been endorsed by nearly 100 organisations and individuals, sets out our shared demands. Our Symposium Policy Brief provides concrete recommendations for advancing them through legal, political and institutional processes.  The Collective will keep carrying these demands into every forum where decisions are made. Repair is a responsibility, and we will not let COP31 turn it into an afterthought. 

By: The African Climate Reparations Collective
